--- OPTION A · Multilingual Health QA (RECOMMENDED) ---

I built a multilingual question-answering system for health queries in low-resource African languages. The challenge: answer user questions in languages like Wolof, Pulaar, French and English where labelled training data is extremely scarce.

What I built: an end-to-end NLP pipeline. I ingested a multilingual health corpus, engineered cross-lingual representations so the model could transfer learning from high-resource languages to under-resourced ones, and added a retrieval step so every answer is grounded in source material instead of being hallucinated by the model.

Techniques used: multilingual embeddings and cross-lingual transfer learning, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for factual grounding, data augmentation to compensate for the scarcity of labelled data, and a structured evaluation loop against a held-out benchmark with error analysis by language.

Result: ranked 69 out of 573 participants — Top 12% — on Zindi's "Multilingual Health Question Answering in Low-Resource African Languages" challenge (2026), competing against teams with far more labelled data.

Why this matters to me: I speak Wolof natively. Building AI that actually works in African languages is not a benchmark exercise for me — it is the entire point.

--- OPTION B · RAG Chatbot (alternative) ---

I built an academic assistant chatbot on a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) architecture. It indexes the school's internal library — student theses and technical reports — and answers questions by retrieving the relevant passages before generating a response, so answers are grounded in real documents rather than invented.

What I built: a full RAG pipeline — document ingestion and chunking, embedding and vector search, retrieval, and answer generation with citations back to the source. I evaluated it on real student questions and iterated on chunk size, retrieval quality and prompt structure until the answers were accurate and citable.

Techniques used: RAG, embeddings and vector similarity search, document chunking strategies, prompt engineering, and Python for the orchestration layer.

Why it matters: it turned a scattered, hard-to-search institutional archive into something students actually use — retrieval instead of hallucination.
